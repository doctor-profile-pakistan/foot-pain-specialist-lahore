What Achilles tendinopathy is

The Achilles is the strongest tendon in the body. It connects the calf muscles to the heel bone and stores energy with each step.

Tendinopathy is a problem with the tendon itself, brought on by load rather than by a single injury. The tendon becomes painful, less tolerant of force, and sometimes visibly thickened.

It is described in two main ways. In the middle part of the tendon, the pain sits a few centimetres above the heel bone. At the insertion, the pain sits directly where the tendon meets the heel. The two behave differently and are treated differently.

It is not the same as a rupture. A rupture is a sudden complete tear, usually with a snap and an inability to push off at all. That is a separate problem and needs urgent assessment.

What it feels like

The pattern is usually more informative than the severity.

  • LocationBack of the ankle, above or at the heel bone
  • MorningStiff and sore for the first steps
  • Warm-upEases once you have been moving for a while
  • After activitySore again the next morning
  • Push-offPain when walking uphill, on stairs or when running
  • AppearanceSometimes a thickened or tender area on the tendon

Why it happens

Almost always a load problem, sometimes combined with tissue quality.

Most cases follow a rise in load. Returning to sport after a break, a longer walking or running routine, a job that involves stairs, or a change of footwear can all raise the demand on the tendon faster than it can adapt.

Tight or weak calf muscles increase the strain on the tendon with every step. Age reduces the tendon's ability to recover between loads, which is why the same training that was tolerated at thirty may not be tolerated at fifty.

Certain medications are associated with tendon problems. This is one of the reasons the assessment includes your current medication rather than the foot alone.

How Achilles tendinopathy is treated here

Load management comes first. Where the pain persists despite it, image-guided treatment is used.

  • Non-surgical
  • Ultrasound guided
  • Outpatient
  • No surgery

Load management

First line

The tendon needs the right amount of load, neither rest nor the full routine that provoked it. What that means for your case, including any exercise programme, is set out at the consultation.

If this is the right treatment for you, you will be advised to follow it rather than being offered an injection.

Image-guided injection

Ultrasound guided

Medication is placed around the tendon, not into it, under ultrasound guidance. Ultrasound shows the tendon, the surrounding sheath and the small vessels that often accompany tendinopathy, which is what allows the needle to be positioned accurately.

Platelet-rich plasma

Regenerative

Platelet-rich plasma is prepared from a sample of your own blood and placed around the tendon to support the repair response. It is used for longer-standing cases, or where an earlier injection gave only short-lived relief.

When to be assessed

Most cases respond to load management. These signs mean it should be looked at.

  1. The pain has lasted more than six weeks despite reducing activity.

  2. It is present at rest, or wakes you at night.

  3. The tendon has become visibly thickened or tender to touch.

  4. You cannot push off the affected foot, or heard a snap at the time of injury.

  5. Both Achilles tendons are affected.

A sudden snap at the back of the ankle, with inability to stand on tiptoe, is not tendinopathy. It needs urgent assessment.

Questions about Achilles pain

For anything not covered here, call the clinic.

What is Achilles tendinopathy?

It is a problem in the Achilles tendon caused by load exceeding what the tendon can adapt to. The tendon becomes painful and less able to tolerate force, particularly during push-off.

Where does Achilles tendon pain usually sit?

Either a few centimetres above the heel bone, in the middle part of the tendon, or directly at the point where the tendon attaches to the heel. The two are assessed and treated slightly differently.

Can Achilles tendinopathy be treated without surgery?

In most cases, yes. Treatment here is non-surgical and includes load management, image-guided injections and platelet-rich plasma.

Is PRP used for the Achilles tendon?

Yes. Platelet-rich plasma prepared from your own blood can be injected around the tendon under image guidance. Suitability is decided at the consultation.

Do I have to stop walking altogether?

Load is usually adjusted rather than removed completely. What that means for your particular case is decided at the consultation, after the tendon has been examined.
