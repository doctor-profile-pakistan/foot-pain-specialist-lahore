What plantar fasciitis is

A thick band of tissue runs from the heel to the base of the toes, supporting the arch of the foot. Plantar fasciitis is irritation of that band.

The plantar fascia takes tension with every step. When the load on it rises faster than the tissue can adapt, small areas of irritation develop, usually where the band attaches to the heel bone.

The result is pain under the heel rather than in the arch, because that attachment point carries the most tension.

It is not a tear in most cases, and it is not caused by a heel spur. A spur can appear on an X-ray in people who have no heel pain at all.

What it feels like

The pattern of the pain is usually more useful than the pain score.

  • LocationUnder the heel, often slightly towards the arch
  • MorningSharp with the first steps out of bed
  • After restReturns after sitting or driving for a period
  • MovementEases after a few minutes of walking, then worsens with prolonged standing
  • CharacterSharp or stabbing rather than dull
  • FootwearUsually worse in flat, thin or hard-soled shoes

Why it happens

It is a load problem more often than an injury.

Most cases follow a change in load. A new job that involves standing, a longer walking routine, a period of weight gain, or a change of footwear can all raise the tension through the fascia faster than it can adapt.

Foot shape contributes. A high arch or a low arch changes how weight is distributed through the band. Tight calf muscles increase the pull on the heel with each step.

Age matters as well. The tissue loses some elasticity over time, so the same load that was tolerated at twenty-five may not be tolerated at forty-five.

How plantar fasciitis is treated here

Conservative care comes first. Where it has already been tried and the pain persists, image-guided treatment is used.

  • Non-surgical
  • Ultrasound & C-Arm guided
  • Outpatient
  • No surgery

Rest, footwear and load

First line

The first step is reducing the load that provoked the problem, and supporting the arch so the band is not under constant tension. Where physiotherapy is appropriate, it is arranged alongside this.

If this is the right treatment for your case, you will be advised to follow it rather than being offered an injection.

Image-guided injection

Ultrasound or C-Arm guided

Medication is placed at the irritated attachment of the fascia, using ultrasound or C-Arm fluoroscopy to confirm the position of the needle. Precise placement is what separates an injection that helps for months from one that helps for a fortnight.

Platelet-rich plasma

Regenerative

Platelet-rich plasma is prepared from a sample of your own blood and injected into the fascia to support the repair response. It is used where the problem has been present for longer, or where an earlier injection gave only short-lived relief.

When to be assessed

Most heel pain settles within a few weeks. These signs mean it should be looked at.

  1. The pain has lasted more than six weeks despite rest and a change of footwear.

  2. It is present at rest, or wakes you at night.

  3. You are unable to put weight on the heel in the morning.

  4. Both feet are affected, or the pain is spreading into the arch.

  5. There is swelling, redness or numbness around the heel.

Pain at the back of the heel rather than underneath it usually points to the Achilles tendon, not the plantar fascia. Pain that spreads into the ankle is assessed differently.

Questions about plantar fasciitis

For anything not covered here, call the clinic.

What does plantar fasciitis feel like?

It is usually a sharp or stabbing pain under the heel, close to the arch. It is worst with the first steps in the morning, eases after a few minutes of walking, and often returns after a period of sitting or standing.

Why is the pain worse in the morning?

The plantar fascia shortens while the foot rests, particularly overnight. The first steps stretch it again, which is why the pain is sharpest then and usually eases as you move.

Is a heel spur the same as plantar fasciitis?

No. A heel spur is a bony change that may be seen on an X-ray. The pain of plantar fasciitis comes from the fascia itself, and many people with a spur have no pain at all.

Can plantar fasciitis be treated without surgery?

Yes. Treatment here is non-surgical and includes rest and footwear advice, physiotherapy where appropriate, image-guided injections and platelet-rich plasma.

Is PRP used for plantar fasciitis?

Yes. Platelet-rich plasma prepared from your own blood can be injected into the plantar fascia under image guidance. Suitability is decided at the consultation.
