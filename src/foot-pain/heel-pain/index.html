Heel Pain Treatment in Lahore

Heel pain has more than one cause, and each is treated differently. The assessment identifies which structure is responsible, so treatment is directed at the right place rather than at the heel as a whole.

Three structures produce most heel pain

Where the pain sits, and what makes it worse, usually indicates which one is involved.

  • The plantar fascia → Sharp pain under the heel, worst on the first steps of the day
  • The Achilles tendon → Pain at the back of the heel, above the bone
  • A nerve around the heel or ankle → Burning or electric pain, sometimes with numbness

The heel pad, which cushions the bone, can also lose volume with age. When that happens the pain is present on standing rather than on the first step, and it is assessed differently from plantar fasciitis.

Reading the pattern

The detail of when and where it hurts is the most useful information you can bring.

  • Under the heelMost often the plantar fascia
  • Back of the heelAchilles tendon or the bursa behind it
  • Burning or electricPoints towards a nerve
  • First stepsTypical of the plantar fascia
  • On standingCan indicate the heel pad
  • Both heelsSuggests a systemic cause, which is assessed separately

None of these is conclusive on its own. They guide the examination, and the examination guides the treatment.

Why it happens

Load, tissue quality and foot shape, usually in combination.

Most heel pain follows a change in load. A new job that involves standing, a longer walking routine, a period of weight gain, or a change of footwear can raise the tension through the tissue faster than it can adapt.

Age matters. The heel pad thins, tendons lose elasticity, and the same load that was tolerated at twenty-five may not be tolerated at fifty.

Foot shape contributes. A high arch or a low arch changes how weight is distributed through the heel. Tight calf muscles increase the pull on both the fascia and the Achilles with each step.

How heel pain is treated here

Conservative care comes first. Where it has already been tried and the pain persists, image-guided treatment is used.

  • Non-surgical
  • Ultrasound & C-Arm guided
  • Outpatient
  • No surgery

Load and footwear

First line

The first step is reducing the load that provoked the problem and supporting the heel so the tissue is not under constant tension. Physiotherapy is arranged where it is appropriate.

If this is the right treatment for your case, you will be advised to follow it rather than being offered an injection.

Image-guided injection

Ultrasound or C-Arm guided

Medication is placed at the specific structure responsible for the pain, using ultrasound or C-Arm fluoroscopy to confirm the needle position. This is what allows the treatment to be aimed at the fascia, the tendon or the nerve rather than at the heel generally.

Nerve block

Where the pain is nerve-related

If the examination suggests a nerve is carrying the pain, a block confirms it and can reduce the pain for an extended period. This also separates nerve pain from tendon pain when the two are difficult to tell apart.

Platelet-rich plasma

Regenerative

Platelet-rich plasma is prepared from a sample of your own blood and injected into damaged tissue to support the repair response. It is used where the problem has been present for longer, or where an earlier injection gave only short-lived relief.

When to be assessed

These signs mean the pain should be looked at rather than waited out.

  1. The pain has lasted more than six weeks despite rest and a change of footwear.

  2. It is present at rest, or wakes you at night.

  3. You are unable to put weight on the heel in the morning.

  4. There is burning, numbness or tingling around the heel.

  5. Both heels are affected, or the pain is spreading.

Questions about heel pain

For anything not covered here, call the clinic.

What causes heel pain?

The most common cause is plantar fasciitis. Other causes include Achilles tendon problems, irritation of a nerve around the heel or ankle, and the heel pad losing some of its cushioning. The location of the pain usually points to the cause.

Is heel pain the same as plantar fasciitis?

No. Plantar fasciitis is the most common cause of heel pain, but it is not the only one. Pain at the back of the heel usually involves the Achilles tendon, and pain that spreads or feels burning can indicate a nerve.

Do I need an X-ray before the consultation?

Not necessarily. Many cases are diagnosed from the history and examination alone, with ultrasound used in the clinic where helpful. If you already have an X-ray or MRI, bring it with you.

Can heel pain be treated without surgery?

In most cases, yes. Treatment here is non-surgical and includes load and footwear advice, physiotherapy where appropriate, image-guided injections and platelet-rich plasma.
