What platelet-rich plasma is

Blood contains platelets, and platelets carry the growth factors involved in tissue repair. PRP concentrates those platelets before returning them to the body.

A small sample of blood is drawn in the clinic and placed in a centrifuge. Spinning separates the blood into layers, and the platelet-rich layer is collected. That concentrate is then injected into the injured tendon, fascia or joint.

The preparation used at this clinic is leukocyte-poor PRP, also written LP-PRP. The white cells are reduced during processing, so the concentrate carries a higher proportion of platelets and growth factors.

Because the material comes from your own blood, there is no donor product involved in standard PRP. Growth factor therapy and exosome therapy are separate preparations, described below.

Foot and ankle conditions treated with PRP

Regenerative therapy is used where tissue is damaged but the structure is still intact. In the foot and ankle that means tendon injuries, ligament sprains, and arthritis of the ankle joint.

  • Plantar fasciitis → Heel pain that is worst on the first steps of the day
  • Heel pain → Pain under the heel that builds through the day
  • Achilles tendinopathy → Pain and stiffness at the back of the ankle
  • Ankle joint pain and sprains → Arthritis, ligament injury and ongoing instability

Regenerative therapies offered

Three preparations are available. The choice is made at assessment, after the damaged structure has been identified.

LP-PRP

Leukocyte-poor platelet-rich plasma

A concentrated form of your own platelets, processed to reduce the white cells. It carries the growth factors associated with tissue repair with a lower inflammatory component.

The concentrate is injected into the affected tendon, fascia or joint under image guidance.

Growth factor therapy

Concentrated signalling proteins

This preparation goes a step beyond standard PRP by further isolating and concentrating specific growth factors involved in new blood vessel formation, collagen production and cellular repair.

It is delivered directly to the site of damage under the same image guidance.

Exosome therapy

Cell signalling vesicles

Exosomes are small signalling vesicles that carry instructions between cells. They are not cells themselves.

A laboratory-prepared exosome product from a certified source is injected into the affected area to support the repair response. Suitability and the expected role of this therapy in your treatment plan are discussed at assessment.

Benefits of PRP therapy

What the treatment offers, stated without overstating what it can do.

Your own blood

No donor product

The preparation is made from a sample of your own blood, drawn in the clinic. In standard PRP no donor material is involved, which removes the question of compatibility.

No surgery, no theatre

Outpatient

The treatment is a minimally invasive injection performed in the clinic. There is no incision and no recovery from an operation, which is why it is considered before surgery rather than after it.

Directed at the damaged tissue

Supporting repair

The concentrate is placed into the structure that is damaged, so the growth factors involved in repair reach the area that needs them. The aim is to support the tissue, not only to dull the sensation of pain.

Used alongside other treatment

Part of a plan

PRP is usually combined with load management or physiotherapy rather than used instead of them. The injection makes movement more comfortable, and the rehabilitation is what changes the load on the tissue.

Response to PRP varies between patients and between conditions. It is not a cure, and it is not a substitute for surgery where surgery is required.

What happens during a PRP procedure

The appointment is completed in the clinic. You will be given written aftercare instructions before you leave.

  1. Assessment. Your history is taken and the foot or ankle is examined. Ultrasound is used where it helps to see the tendon, fascia or joint directly. Suitability for regenerative therapy is decided here.

  2. Blood sample. A small sample of blood is drawn in the clinic.

  3. Preparation. The sample is centrifuged and the platelet-rich layer is separated and concentrated.

  4. Injection. The concentrate is placed into the target structure under ultrasound or C-Arm guidance.

  5. Aftercare. Instructions for the following days are given in writing, and a review is arranged if needed.

How long PRP takes to work

PRP does not act like a painkiller. It supports a repair process, and that process runs over weeks and months rather than hours.

  1. First few days

    Temporary soreness

    Mild discomfort or a short-lived increase in soreness at the injection site is common. It settles, and it reflects the response beginning rather than a problem with the procedure.

  2. Three to four weeks

    First change in pain

    The initial improvement is usually noticed around this point. Commonly, movement becomes easier before the pain itself fully settles.

  3. Three to six months

    Tissue remodelling

    The repair continues well after the pain has changed. This is the period over which the tissue consolidates, which is why progress is judged over months rather than days.

What influences the timeline

  • ConditionA longstanding tendon problem behaves differently from a recent injury
  • SeverityHow much tissue is affected, and for how long it has been affected
  • General healthAge and circulation affect the repair response
  • LoadWhether the tissue is loaded appropriately alongside treatment

These ranges describe what is typically reported. Response varies with the condition, how long it has been present, and your general health. They are not a guarantee for your case, and your progress is reviewed at follow-up.

What PRP does not do

An honest account of the limits, so you can decide with clear expectations.

PRP supports the body's repair response. It does not replace it, and it is not a cure.

Response varies between patients and between conditions. Some people notice improvement over a period of weeks, and some do not. Where a structural problem requires surgery, PRP is not a substitute for that surgery.

Not everyone is suitable. Suitability depends on your history and current medication, and is decided at the consultation rather than in advance.

If rest, a change of footwear, or physiotherapy is the appropriate treatment for your case, you will be advised accordingly instead of being offered an injection.

Questions about PRP

If your question is not here, call the clinic directly.

What is PRP made from?

PRP is made from a sample of your own blood. The sample is centrifuged to separate and concentrate the platelets, and the concentrate is then injected into the damaged tissue.

Which foot conditions are treated with PRP?

At this clinic PRP is used for plantar fasciitis, heel pain, Achilles tendinopathy, ankle joint pain and ankle sprains. Suitability for your particular case is decided at the consultation.

Is the injection guided by imaging?

Yes. Injections are placed under ultrasound or C-Arm fluoroscopy guidance so that the concentrate reaches the specific tendon, fascia or joint being treated.

Is PRP a surgical procedure?

No. PRP is a minimally invasive injection performed in the clinic on an outpatient basis. No surgery is involved.

How many PRP sessions will I need?

The number of injections depends on the condition being treated and on how you respond. This is discussed with you at the consultation, after the assessment.
