Why the guidance matters

A joint space in the ankle is a few millimetres wide. Anatomy alone does not reliably show where the needle tip is.

Without imaging, an injection relies on surface landmarks and on how the tissue feels as the needle passes. That works often enough to be common practice, but it does not confirm where the medication actually went.

With imaging, the physician sees the target and the needle at the same time. If the tip is not where it should be, that is corrected before anything is injected.

This is why the same medication can behave very differently depending on how it is placed. An injection that misses the inflamed tissue produces a short-lived result, not because the medication failed but because it was never delivered to the right place.

Two forms of guidance

The choice depends on the structure being treated, not on preference.

Ultrasound

Soft tissue, in real time

Ultrasound shows tendon, fascia, ligament and muscle as the needle moves. It is the appropriate guidance for plantar fascia, the Achilles tendon and the soft tissue around the ankle.

It also allows the physician to see inflammation and small vessels alongside the structure being treated.

C-Arm fluoroscopy

Bone and joint spaces

Fluoroscopy shows bone and the inside of a joint. It is used where the target is a joint space, or where the needle must pass close to bone.

Contrast dye is sometimes used to confirm that the needle is inside the joint before the medication is given.

What it treats

Conditions of the foot and ankle where a specific structure can be identified as the source of pain.

  • Plantar fasciitis → Injection at the irritated attachment of the fascia
  • Heel pain → Directed at the structure the assessment identifies
  • Achilles tendinopathy → Placement around the tendon under ultrasound
  • Ankle joint pain → Placement inside the joint under C-Arm guidance

What happens during the procedure

The appointment is completed in the clinic. You will be given aftercare instructions before you leave.

  1. Assessment. The history and examination identify the structure to be treated. Ultrasound is used here to confirm the target.

  2. Positioning. The foot or ankle is positioned so the target can be seen clearly on the image.

  3. Needle placement. The needle is advanced while the image is watched, until the tip is at the target.

  4. Injection. The medication is given once the position is confirmed.

  5. Aftercare. Instructions for the following days are given in writing, and a review is arranged if needed.

What an injection can and cannot do

An injection is a tool, not a cure. Where it sits in your treatment plan is decided at the consultation.

An injection can reduce pain and inflammation at a specific structure. That can make movement and rehabilitation possible again, which is often the point of doing it.

It does not change the load that caused the problem, and it does not repair a tendon or a joint by itself. If the underlying cause is not addressed, the pain can return.

It also does not replace surgery where surgery is genuinely required. Where a structure is beyond what an injection can help, that is said plainly at the consultation rather than discovered after several attempts.

Not every case requires a procedure. If rest, a change of footwear, or physiotherapy is the appropriate treatment, you will be advised accordingly.

Questions about image-guided injections

For anything not covered here, call the clinic.

What does image-guided mean?

It means the needle is positioned while the physician watches an image of the area, either ultrasound or C-Arm fluoroscopy, rather than relying on touch and anatomy alone. The target structure and the needle tip are seen at the same time.

What is the difference between ultrasound and C-Arm guidance?

Ultrasound shows soft tissue such as tendon, fascia and ligament in real time. C-Arm fluoroscopy shows bone and the inside of a joint. The choice depends on which structure is being treated.

Is an image-guided injection the same as a steroid injection?

Not necessarily. Image guidance describes how the needle is placed, not what is injected. What is injected is decided at the consultation, and it is discussed with you before the procedure.

How long does the appointment take?

The procedure itself is short and is performed in the clinic on an outpatient basis. The total time depends on the assessment and on the structure being treated, and is confirmed when you book.

Do I need imaging before I come?

No. Bring any X-rays, MRI films or reports you already have. Further imaging is requested only where the assessment indicates it is needed.
